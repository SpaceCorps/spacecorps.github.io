47,000× faster electron emission: what we built for a client, and how it compares to GETELEC

SpaceCorps · 16 October 2026 · Case study · Physics & simulation

A client needed to compute electron emission from metal nanotips across millions of field, geometry and temperature combinations. The reference tool took about 650 ms per point. We built spaceemit in Rust: 17 µs per point, with the same answers.

The problem

Cold field emission, thermionic emission and everything between them: when a metal tip sits in a strong electric field, electrons tunnel out through the surface barrier. The current density J and the Nottingham heat PN (the energy the emission carries away or deposits) depend on the local field, tip radius, work function and temperature, and the relationship is highly non-linear. Coupled simulations of a heating tip need that answer for every surface atom at every coupling step. At 650 ms per point, that is out of reach.

What we built

spaceemit is a Rust library and HTTP service that computes J, PN and energy spectra with numerically exact transmission through the barrier (no analytic shortcut such as Murphy–Good). It was written clean-room: its behaviour comes from the published physics and black-box reference data, never from another program's source. It sits inside the same engine family as our molecular dynamics code, spacemd, and the coupled nanotip simulation spacetip.

Benchmarks

We compared spaceemit 0.2.0 against the 2026 Python/numba edition of GETELEC (S. Barranco Cárceles et al.) on 2,456 metal emission points, on one Apple M4 Max with 16 threads, on 5 October 2026.

Do the answers match?

Speed means nothing if the physics drifts. Over the 2,199 points with J ≥ 10−20 A/nm², the median |ΔJ| between the two programs is 0.0007 % (90th percentile 0.003 %, worst case 0.4 %), and the median |ΔPN| is 0.003 %. The sign of the Nottingham heat agrees at every point across the field, intermediate and thermionic regimes.

Fair-comparison notes. GETELEC was run with the settings shipped in its own examples and was not tuned; coarser grids would be faster and less accurate. GETELEC solves a more general problem (including semiconductor emitters, which we did not compare), and its timings are in-process after JIT warm-up. Both programs agree closely with each other, and both differ from an older reference data set by the same median 1.5 %. We are not claiming that GETELEC is badly written; spaceemit is a purpose-built solver for one problem. Full method and raw data are in the benchmarks/getelec folder of the engine repository.

Why it matters

A solver that takes microseconds changes what you can do: a live viewer where you drag the applied field and watch a copper tip heat up, parameter sweeps over millions of points in seconds, or fitting measured I–V curves interactively. That is what the spacetip coupled simulation does: molecular dynamics, a field solver and emission physics in one loop.
